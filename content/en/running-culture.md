## Race etiquette is not decoration

Rules differ by event. The Boston Marathon rules, for example, state that bibs are non-transferable, participants may not run with anyone not registered as an official participant on any part of the course, headphone use must leave runners aware of their surroundings, and professional, wheelchair and para athletics divisions have stricter limits. All participants must also follow the event code of conduct.

The purpose is safety, fairness and respect for other runners. Read the rules before entering; do not stop suddenly in the middle of the course, cut across others, run with someone else's bib or drop gel wrappers on the course. Headphone, pacer and aid rules vary between races, so do not carry one event's habits into another.

## Records and course conditions

World Athletics publishes world records by event and marks records that are pending ratification; road-running tables also distinguish mixed-gender and women-only races. A record is a governing-body recognition, not simply a legendary “fastest time”.

When comparing performances, consider course certification, elevation change, weather, start conditions and record category. The marathon is fixed at 42.195 km, but course difficulty and conditions vary enormously; comparing two finish times without context easily produces the wrong conclusion.

## How to read running research

A systematic review pooled 36 studies and 23,047 runners and reported that about 26.2% had a running-related injury. Yet the evidence linking weekly distance, frequency, intensity or recent changes to injury was inconsistent, and the authors cautioned against recommending one “optimal progression” for everyone.

When reading a study, start with the design, sample size, population, measurements and the limitations the authors acknowledge. Observational studies can show associations but not prove causation; one small study should not overturn long-term practice. Put the result into the wider evidence base rather than letting one headline dictate training.

## Gear and technology narratives

Shoe, plate, watch and supplement marketing often attributes complex performance to a single product. A physiology review reminds us that marathon performance is multi-factorial, involving physiological, technological and cognitive determinants; VO2max, lactate threshold and running economy are also protocol-dependent and should not be treated as interchangeable measurements.

Gear can improve comfort, safety or efficiency, but no product replaces training, recovery and individual adaptation. When a claim promises a percentage improvement, ask about the study population, comparison conditions, funding and practical meaning.

## Scope and limits

This page does not issue final record rulings, interpret a race's rules for you or recommend gear. Event rules belong to the organiser, records belong to World Athletics, and research questions belong with the original papers and statistical methods rather than second-hand summaries.
