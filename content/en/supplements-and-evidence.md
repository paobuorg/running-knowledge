## Supplements are not medicines

The U.S. FDA regulates dietary supplements differently from prescription drugs. Products do not need pre-market approval for efficacy and safety in the way drugs do; companies are responsible for labelling and safety, and the FDA mainly acts after products reach the market. Being available does not mean proven effective or safe for everyone.

A supplement label may list ingredients without full doses or third-party testing information, and actual content can differ between batches. Choosing products with third-party testing and transparent ingredients and doses reduces some risk, but does not guarantee benefit.

## Ask what is missing first

For most runners, a balanced diet supplies most of what training needs. Whether protein, carbohydrate, iron, vitamin D or anything else needs supplementing depends on diet, testing, health and training load, not on “runners should take it”. Without a clear deficiency or professional advice, stacking supplements usually adds little benefit while increasing cost and interaction risk.

The ISSN protein position stand supports reasonable protein distribution when total protein and energy are adequate; that is not proof that everyone needs protein powder. Caffeine has moderate-to-large effects in some endurance settings, but dose, sleep and anxiety limits apply; see the caffeine article.

## Levels of evidence

Randomised trials, systematic reviews and meta-analyses are more reliable than a single small study. Supplement research often has small samples, funding issues and differences in dose and population. When a product claims a percentage improvement, ask who was studied, what the comparison was, whether the difference matters and whether it has been replicated. Cell and animal studies, or an “expert recommendation”, do not directly prove benefit for runners.

Be especially cautious of products claiming to treat disease, replace medication or guarantee performance. Supplements do not replace medical diagnosis, treatment or a balanced diet. With a medical condition, pregnancy, breastfeeding, medication or a history of disordered eating, consult a doctor or registered dietitian first.

## Racing and anti-doping

Do not try a new supplement for the first time on race day. Some ingredients may be restricted by anti-doping rules or contaminated during manufacture; being sold in a normal shop does not make it permitted in competition. Check the latest prohibited list from the event and anti-doping authority, and keep batch and purchase records.

## Scope and limits

This page does not recommend a brand, dose or stack, or decide whether a product suits you. Supplements can interact with medication and burden the liver, kidneys, cardiovascular system or gut; stop and seek medical advice for unusual symptoms.
