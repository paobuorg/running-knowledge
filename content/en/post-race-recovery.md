## Three things after the finish

After finishing, cool down by walking for a few minutes instead of stopping abruptly. Then take fluid and carbohydrate, adding protein if appetite allows; if solid food is difficult, a carbohydrate-electrolyte drink is a start. The first meal does not need to be a feast; the priority is energy and fluid.

Go home, shower, change into dry clothes and sleep. Immune function, sleep and muscle repair all need time; celebrations, long drives and alcohol can wait. Get warm quickly in cold weather; in heat, keep drinking and avoid prolonged sun exposure.

## Soreness, pain and warning signs

Delayed-onset muscle soreness usually appears a day or two after a race, with tenderness, stiffness and reduced strength, especially on stairs. It is different from an acute injury. Seek medical care for sharp pain, swelling, inability to bear weight, a locked joint, numbness, fever, chest pain, fainting or unusual palpitations rather than trying to “flush out” the race.

A fall, sprain or head impact during the race deserves attention even if you could continue at the time. Worsening headache, vomiting, confusion or drowsiness after a head impact needs urgent medical assessment.

## Returning to running

There is no universal timetable. After a 5K or 10K, many runners can jog easily within a few days; after a marathon or ultramarathon, recovery is usually measured in weeks. The useful criteria are not the calendar but whether walking is normal, pain has settled, sleep and energy are back, and an easy run actually feels easy.

Start with short walks and easy running every other day, and check the next-day response. If pain worsens, movement compensates or fatigue persists, go back a step. Do not use one “recovery run” as proof that you are recovered, and do not schedule quality sessions or long runs immediately after a race.

## Food and sleep

In the 24–48 hours after a race, normal meals matter more than precise calculations. Include carbohydrate, protein and vegetables at meals and drink to thirst; if sweat losses were high, include an electrolyte drink. Alcohol can impair sleep and recovery, so a large amount on race night is unhelpful.

Sleep is the most easily ignored recovery tool. Keep regular hours for a few days and nap briefly if needed. Persistent insomnia, low mood or fatigue lasting several weeks deserves professional assessment.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide an individual rehabilitation plan, injury diagnosis or medication advice. Race distance, surface, weather and personal health vary widely; people with a medical condition, pregnancy, previous injury or persistent post-race symptoms should consult a doctor or physiotherapist.
