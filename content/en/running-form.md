## Observe before changing

Running technique is not one fixed posture. Cadence, stride length, foot strike and arm swing change with speed, gradient, fatigue and surface. Before chasing a “standard” form, record your natural rhythm in different conditions and decide whether a small change is actually useful.

## Cadence is not a 180 exam

A systematic review found that, at a fixed speed, a higher stride rate generally reduced vertical centre-of-mass excursion, ground reaction force and joint energy absorption — biomechanical factors associated with some running injuries. The included studies were small and methodologically varied, so they do not support “everyone should run at 180 steps per minute”. Treat 180 as a reference, not a target.

A practical approach is to count steps for 30 seconds during easy runs, repeat over several sessions, and watch how the number changes with fatigue, speed and gradient. If you experiment with a higher cadence, change it by about 5% at a time, keep speed constant, and give it two or three weeks before judging comfort. Stop if pain appears.

## Breathing is not a fixed beat

Breathing changes with speed, gradient, fatigue and personal habit. There is no universal “inhale for three steps, exhale for two” that everyone must follow. Forcing a fixed rhythm can increase shoulder and neck tension and add unnecessary effort. The talk test is more useful for intensity: an easy run should allow full sentences; if you can only manage a few words, the effort is already high — slow down or shorten the stride.

Nasal or mouth breathing are both acceptable; the key is meeting ventilation demand. There is no need to force nose-only breathing to look “more professional”. If you are clearly short of breath at an easy effort, or have chest tightness, wheeze or dizziness, stop and seek medical advice.

## Stride length mostly follows speed

Stride length is not a number to “pull” larger by itself. As speed rises, both cadence and stride length usually increase; deliberately overstriding can put the landing farther ahead, increasing braking and joint load. Decide the speed first and let stride length follow, rather than using a long stride to chase speed.

## Uphill and downhill are different skills

Research shows that uphill running usually has a higher step frequency and shorter flight time, with greater hip muscular work. Downhill running has longer flight time, lower step frequency and more eccentric load. Shorten the stride and keep breathing easy uphill; control speed downhill instead of “letting it go” and losing landing control. There is no need to copy a flat-road rhythm onto a climb.

## Surface matters, with limits

One small study compared artificial grass, rubber and concrete and found no significant differences in many joint-motion and loading variables, but higher ankle moments and frontal-plane hip/knee moments on concrete. A single study cannot produce a universal rule, but it is a reminder to reduce intensity and observe your response when the surface is hard and fatigue accumulates.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose individual form, treat injury or prescribe a “correct” posture for every runner. Persistent pain, a new injury or a history of injury deserves assessment by a qualified medical or sports professional. Technique work cannot replace sleep, load management and recovery.

## Sources and scope

The cadence/load relationship follows a Sports Health systematic review; hill differences follow a Sports Medicine review; surface differences follow a small PLOS ONE study; breathing and intensity judgement follow the CDC talk test. The studies have sample and design limits; this page turns them into an observation framework, not a guarantee.
