## Start with the daily total

Recovery after training, daytime energy and race performance depend first on whether daily energy intake is sufficient. One low day will not ruin a training block, but consistently eating less than you expend can show up as persistent fatigue, repeated injury, feeling cold, poor sleep or losing interest in training. When those signs appear, check total intake and recovery before adding supplements.

You do not need to count every calorie. For most amateur runners, a more practical approach is to watch weight trends, training feel, hunger and recovery speed, and adjust food together with training load.

## Carbohydrate and protein

Carbohydrate is the main fuel for endurance exercise. Staple grains, fruit, legumes and potatoes provide it, and intake usually needs to rise with training volume. Protein supports repair and muscle maintenance. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand considers 1.4–2.0 g/kg body weight per day sufficient for most exercising people, with servings of about 20–40 g every 3–4 hours.

These are population ranges, not exact targets everyone must hit. Ordinary meals usually meet protein needs; protein powder is a convenient option, not a requirement. Endurance runners should not squeeze out enough carbohydrate in pursuit of high protein.

## Weight management is not a race-week project

Weight is influenced by sleep, age, genes, medication and overall energy balance. The CDC notes that even active people can gain weight if intake consistently exceeds expenditure; conversely, suddenly eating much less before a race is not a reliable performance strategy.

If weight change is a goal, make it gradual, preferably when training load is lower, and avoid extreme diets before a key race. Unexplained weight change, illness or medication effects deserve assessment by a clinician or registered dietitian.

## Caffeine and supplements

The ISSN review reports that caffeine at 3–6 mg/kg body weight can improve endurance performance in many people, with a common timing of about 60 minutes before exercise. Responses vary, and later intake can disturb sleep or worsen anxiety and palpitations. Treat caffeine as a variable to test, not a fixed race-day ritual.

Supplements are not drugs. The FDA regulates dietary supplements differently from medicines: manufacturers are responsible for evaluating safety and labelling before marketing, while the FDA can act after products reach the market. Most runners do not need supplements if their diet is adequate. If you consider one, check the ingredients, dose and third-party testing information, and discuss it with a clinician or dietitian — especially with a medical condition, medication use or pregnancy.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not prescribe individual calorie, weight-loss or supplement plans, and does not diagnose eating disorders, anaemia or endocrine problems. Persistent fatigue, marked weight change, menstrual changes, repeated stress injuries or a difficult relationship with food deserve help from a clinician or registered dietitian.

## Sources and scope

Protein ranges follow the ISSN protein and exercise position stand; caffeine dose and individual variation follow the ISSN caffeine and exercise performance position stand; weight factors follow CDC healthy-weight guidance; supplement regulation follows the FDA dietary supplements page. This is general nutrition education, not clinical assessment.
