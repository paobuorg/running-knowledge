## What advanced footwear means

“Advanced footwear technology” usually combines a highly resilient foam with a full-length carbon-fibre plate. The aim is to increase longitudinal bending stiffness, alter ankle and metatarsophalangeal mechanics, and potentially lower the metabolic cost of running. It is not magic and does not replace training. The plate is only one part of a shoe; foam, last, drop, weight and your foot shape all affect the experience.

## What the research says

A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that carbon-plated shoes were associated with lower metabolic demand, suggesting some runners may find the same speed less costly. The authors also noted that whether the plate itself produces the metabolic benefit remains debated, and that study design, running speed and individual responses vary. A group average is not a promise for every runner.

Biomechanical studies have not shown every joint load falling uniformly; results differ by shoe and runner. When a claim promises a percentage improvement, ask about the study population, speed, comparison shoe and funding.

## Not everyone gets faster

The benefit of carbon plates varies with pace, cadence, foot shape and adaptation. For some runners they may help in a race or quality session; for others they simply feel stiffer, less stable or more expensive. There is no evidence that they prevent every running injury, and wearing them does not remove the need for load management.

If you try a pair, use them first on an easy run or short quality session and watch the ankle, plantar foot, calf and overall stability. Do not wear new shoes for the first time on race day.

## Training shoes and racing shoes

Training and racing shoes can be separated, but neither “always rotate” nor “always use one pair” is supported for everyone. Rotation may spread wear and give foam time to recover; it may also simply help you manage different surfaces and distances. The key is that each pair fits, feels stable and supports repeatable training. Record mileage and body response.

Carbon-plate durability depends on foam, outsole, body weight and surface. There is no universal “replace at X kilometres” number. Worn-through outsole, collapsed foam, a torn upper or persistent discomfort are more useful signals than mileage alone.

## When to replace

Check the outsole, toe box, heel and upper. Consider replacement when grip is clearly reduced, foam has lost its resilience, wear is markedly asymmetric or the shoe is causing new pain. NHS running-injury advice also emphasises fit, support and not running through significant pain; expensive or high-tech does not automatically mean suitable.

Treat shoes as a variable: record which pair you used, on what surface and whether it caused discomfort. That is more useful than chasing the newest model.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not recommend brands or models, or diagnose foot, ankle or knee problems. Persistent pain, numbness, swelling or a change in gait deserves assessment by a qualified medical or sports professional.

## Sources and scope

Metabolic and biomechanical evidence for carbon-plated shoes follows a systematic review and meta-analysis; shoe comfort and injury associations follow a secondary analysis; shoe and pain advice follows the NHS. The studies have sample and design limits; this page provides a selection and testing framework, not a guarantee.
