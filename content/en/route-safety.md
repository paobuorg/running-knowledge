## A route file is not a safe route

A GPX or map records route geometry, and some files also carry elevation, timestamps or waypoints. It does not tell you whether a road is closed today, whether water is available, whether your phone has coverage, whether the ground is passable after dark, or whether traffic, weather and terrain make the plan sensible. Treat the route file as a planning base and the current race or land-manager notice as the source of truth.

## Leave a trip plan

The U.S. National Park Service recommends giving a trusted person who is not on the trip the route, contacts, expected arrival and return times and companions. Keep an offline map and backup contact details on your phone; in areas without coverage, consider a locator beacon and conserve battery. “Someone knows I went running” is not enough if the route and timing are vague.

## Weather and heat

Check the forecast and warnings before leaving, and prepare alternatives for lightning, heavy rain, flooding or heat. Heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache or fainting can indicate heat exhaustion; if there is vomiting, worsening symptoms or symptoms lasting over an hour, cool the person and seek medical help. Confusion, slurred speech, very high body temperature, a rapid strong pulse or loss of consciousness can indicate heat stroke: call local emergency services immediately and do not simply tell the person to keep going. Heat stroke is not managed by continuing to run or drinking water on your own.

## Night and visibility

Use a headlamp with spare batteries at night, prefer known routes with escape points, wear reflective or high-visibility clothing and tell someone your plan. For solo night runs, make “check in on time” a firm checkpoint, not an option. Do not descend technical terrain in poor visibility just to hit a pace target.

## Water, kit and company

The National Park Service recommends carrying enough water for the conditions and considering turning back when half is gone. For trails and long runs, also carry a map/compass, headlamp, whistle, first aid, extra warmth and basic repair kit. GPS can fail; do not rely only on a watch or phone. Running with someone, shortening the route and turning around early are valid safety decisions.

## The limits of a GPX

This page does not provide live closure, aid, rescue or access-authorisation information for a specific route. Land-manager rules differ, and private land or protected areas may be closed. Before using any track, confirm its licence, whether the route is open, the day's weather and the local emergency number.

## Sources and scope

Heat recognition follows the U.S. National Weather Service; trip planning, weather and navigation follow the U.S. National Park Service Hike Smart and Hiking Safety Tips pages. Emergency numbers and medical care vary by country; this page does not replace local rescue training.
