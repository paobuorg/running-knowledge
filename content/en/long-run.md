## What the long run is for

The long run develops tolerance for more sustained movement: aerobic endurance, fuelling rhythm, route judgement and movement control under fatigue. It is not “the farther the more serious”, and it is not a weekly maximum test. It matters for marathon or trail preparation; for 5K, 10K or health-focused running, a longer but controlled easy run may be enough.

## Length is relative to your base

A sensible long-run length depends on current weekly volume, goal distance, surface and recovery capacity. A common approach is to make it the longer easy run of the week, not to double distance suddenly. If you cannot yet complete shorter runs consistently, stabilise frequency and easy-run time before adding length. A coach who knows your history should adjust specific distances.

## Practise fuelling and route judgement together

The long run is where you test fuelling, fluids and kit. The ISSN position stand says the timing and amount of carbohydrate around exercise can affect performance and recovery, but guidance must fit the exercise demand, overall diet and individual tolerance. The NHS also notes that long physical activity or hot conditions can increase fluid needs. Do not try a new gel, drink or breakfast for the first time on race day.

Choose routes with water, escape points, low traffic risk and familiarity. A GPX helps with geometry only; it does not show today's closures, water or phone coverage. Check the weather and leave a trip plan.

## Recovery boundaries

A systematic review of training parameters and running injury found conflicting evidence for weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity and recent changes. It cannot give every runner one “safe increase”. Do not use a fixed percentage to prove safety; use fatigue, sleep, pain and movement quality across several sessions.

Follow a long run with an easy day or rest. If you are still clearly fatigued the next day, pain worsens or sleep deteriorates, keep or reduce the next long run instead of adding more. Training is a repeatable process, not a one-off assignment.

## When to shorten or cancel

Do not force a long run with fever, significant pain, unusual palpitations or chest pain, dizziness, or persistent vomiting or diarrhoea; address those signals first and seek medical help when needed. In severe weather, on unfamiliar routes or without reliable fuelling, shortening or postponing is more sensible than pushing through.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not prescribe an individual long-run distance or promise that a distance improves performance or prevents injury. A plan should account for your health, injury history, goal and recovery; consult a qualified clinician if you have a medical condition or persistent symptoms.

## Sources and scope

Fuelling principles follow the ISSN nutrient-timing position stand and NHS hydration guidance; the conflicting training-parameter evidence follows a Journal of Athletic Training systematic review. The studies have limits; this page offers a decision framework.
