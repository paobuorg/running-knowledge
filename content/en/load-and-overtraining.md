## Fatigue and adaptation

Fatigue after training is normal. Feeling heavy for hours or days after a long run or quality session, sleeping more deeply, or running slower on an easy day does not automatically mean the training was wrong. Adaptation happens between stimulus and recovery: enough stress with enough recovery can make you stronger; persistent stress with insufficient recovery accumulates fatigue.

The useful question is not “am I tired?” but whether fatigue settles in a reasonable time and whether performance, mood, sleep and health are changing together.

## From functional overload to overtraining

Sports medicine often distinguishes ordinary fatigue, functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching and overtraining syndrome (OTS). Functional overreaching can recover with rest and may contribute to adaptation; when load persistently exceeds recovery, performance may decline, fatigue can last for weeks, and sleep and mood may change.

OTS has no single blood test or imaging finding that confirms it. Assessment is clinical and requires excluding other causes. Thyroid disease, anaemia, infection, low energy intake, sleep disorders and depression can all look like fatigue and declining performance. A watch metric or one bad run cannot diagnose it.

## There is no universal load formula

Weekly distance, intensity, frequency and recent changes are all associated with running injury, but a systematic review found the evidence inconsistent. No single “safe percentage increase” applies to everyone. The 10% rule can be a discussion point, not a law of physiology. A sudden jump in intensity, reduced sleep or extra life stress can make a previously tolerable load intolerable.

Load is not one number. Distance, duration, intensity, gradient, surface, shoes, sleep and life stress belong in the same picture; watching only weekly mileage gives a narrow view.

## Record trends, not single readings

A training log should at least record session type, duration or distance, RPE, pain, sleep and how the day felt. Review the trend weekly: are easy runs getting slower? Is the same quality session costing more effort? Are resting heart rate, sleep and mood worsening together? These patterns carry more information than one day's heart-rate or pace reading.

When the watch and your body disagree, address persistent bodily signals first. A device can show a trend; it cannot replace rest, food and medical assessment.

## When to reduce load or seek help

Reduce volume and intensity, and review sleep, energy intake, illness and life stress, when you notice:

- performance falling or plateauing for weeks, not just one bad session;
- fatigue that does not settle with rest, or unusual effort during light activity;
- marked changes in sleep, mood, appetite or ability to work;
- repeated infections, slow wound healing or persistently abnormal resting heart rate;
- chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or persistent palpitations.

The first four deserve a prompt discussion with a clinician or sports-medicine professional; the last group needs urgent medical assessment. Do not use caffeine, painkillers or “one more week” to mask worsening signals.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not diagnose overtraining syndrome, anaemia, thyroid disease, depression or infection, and does not prescribe a recovery timeline or supplements. Persistent fatigue and performance decline have many possible causes and need assessment by someone who knows your training and health.
