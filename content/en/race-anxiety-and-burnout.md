## Pre-race nerves are not automatically an anxiety disorder

The NHS notes that anxiety is a common response to stress and that occasional worry does not mean generalised anxiety disorder (GAD). GAD is more likely when worry is widespread, persistent and difficult to control, and affects daily life. Symptoms can include sleep difficulty, restlessness, irritability, fatigue, muscle tension, stomach problems and palpitations.

A faster heartbeat, repeated kit checks or poor sleep before a race are familiar to many runners. If worry lasts for weeks, affects work or sleep, or makes normal training and life difficult, consider talking to a clinician or mental-health professional. Adults in the UK can also refer themselves to NHS talking therapies.

## Small actions that help

Rather than telling yourself to “just stop worrying”, put attention on actions: prepare kit and route in advance, use a familiar warm-up, choose a few specific cues (“relax the first 5 km”, “shorten the stride uphill”), and allow yourself to feel nervous.

Avoid adding new caffeine, supplements or rituals on race day; they can amplify palpitations and anxiety. Writing worries down, talking to a friend, or using a familiar breathing or attention practice is usually more useful than repeatedly refreshing race predictions.

## Training burnout is not laziness

WHO lists “burn-out” as an occupational phenomenon and states that it is not classified as a medical condition and should not be applied to other areas of life. Runners' “training burnout” is not a formal diagnosis; it may combine insufficient sleep, low energy intake, life stress, repetitive training and illness.

If persistent exhaustion, loss of interest, a clear performance drop, worsening sleep or low mood lasts more than a week or two, reduce training volume and intensity, review sleep, food and stress, and consider medical or psychological assessment. Do not use “push through” to mask worsening signals.

## Life load and goals

Work, family, caregiving and financial stress all affect training. Goals can change: treating a race as an experience, shortening the distance, deferring it, or simply maintaining health for a while are all legitimate choices. Rest is not failure, and one race does not define your worth.

If training has become persistent self-criticism, secrecy or compulsion, take it seriously. Talking with someone you trust, a clinician or a mental-health professional is safer than struggling alone.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not diagnose anxiety, depression, eating disorders or overtraining, and does not provide psychotherapy or medication advice. If you or someone else is at risk of self-harm, severely hopeless or in a mental-health crisis, contact local emergency or crisis services rather than waiting to adjust a training plan.
