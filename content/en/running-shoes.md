## Fit first, technology second

The first test is fit: room in front of the toes, no pressure across the top of the foot, and no heel sliding while walking or jogging. The NHS suggests getting fitted at a running shop but also notes that you do not have to spend a lot; expensive shoes may mainly be more durable or lighter, and beginners may not need them.

## Cushioning is a perception, not a leaderboard

A secondary analysis of a randomised trial involving 527 recreational runners found that higher perceived heel cushioning and overall shoe appreciation were associated with lower injury risk. That is an association, not proof of cause, and the study had a commercial-funding context. It does not prove that “softer shoes prevent injury”. Choose by comfort, stability and fit during walking, easy jogging and changes of direction, not by a spec sheet or someone else’s recommendation.

## When to replace

No fixed mileage applies to everyone. The NHS lists unsupportive or worn-out shoes among possible contributors to heel pain. Consider replacing shoes when the midsole feels collapsed, the outsole is worn through, the upper no longer holds the foot, or a familiar shoe starts making familiar movements uncomfortable. Record purchase date and use case rather than blindly following a mileage rule.

## Trail shoes are not road shoes

Road shoes prioritise comfort and stability on hard surfaces. Trail running adds mud, rock, wet ground and long hiking sections. Grip, protection and drainage usually cost weight and road feel. Do not treat a road shoe as a universal trail shoe, and do not rely on the outsole alone on technical terrain.

## A minimum trail kit

The U.S. National Park Service “Ten Essentials” is a hiking framework: navigation, sun protection, clothing, illumination, first aid, fire, repair kit, food, water and water-treatment supplies, and shelter. Adapt it to the run's length, weather and escape options, but do not omit a headlamp with spare batteries, basic first aid, extra warmth and reliable navigation just because the activity is “only a run”. Gear cannot replace route judgement.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not recommend brands or decide whether a shoe suits your foot shape, injury history or race goal. Persistent pain, structural foot problems or conditions such as diabetes deserve assessment by a qualified medical or podiatry professional; changing shoes is not treatment.

## Sources and scope

Fit, price and wear guidance follows the NHS running-injury page; the cushioning/injury association follows a secondary European Journal of Sport Science analysis of a randomised trial; the minimum trail kit follows the U.S. National Park Service. The evidence has limits and is not an individual prescription.
