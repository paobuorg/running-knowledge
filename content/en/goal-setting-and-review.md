## More than one kind of goal

Sport psychology often separates outcome goals (finish, placing, time), performance goals (your own pace, distance or technical marker) and process goals (what you do each week, how you sleep, how you fuel). Outcome goals give direction, but weather, course, competitors and daily condition affect them. Process goals are closer to what you control.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of goal setting in sport found a medium, positive effect on task performance, moderated by factors such as goal type, proximity, experience and feedback. It also noted mixed evidence for some psychological outcomes. Goals are not magic; their value comes mainly from directing attention and action toward specific tasks.

## Break it down to this week

“Run under 3:30 this year” is an outcome goal, but it does not tell you what to do today. It can become: one long run, one quality session, two easy runs and one strength session each week; a taper starting four weeks out; a weekly note on sleep and pain. A process goal should specify frequency, duration or a completion standard, not “a bit more” or “try harder”.

Make the goal challenging but not built on unverified assumptions. For a first marathon, “finish steadily, stay injury-free and recover afterwards” is more reasonable than copying an elite runner’s pace. The ODPHP activity planner follows a similar logic: choose activities and goals, then print a weekly tracking plan.

## Review, do not put yourself on trial

Spend five minutes each week: what was done, what was missed and why; how fatigue, sleep, pain, work and stress changed; whether next week should stay the same, reduce or increase. Review is for adjusting the plan, not scoring willpower. Two consecutive weeks of missed sessions usually means the goal is too large, life load has changed or recovery is insufficient — not that you are lazy.

Once a month, review the longer direction: is the race still worth doing, is the date realistic, should the course or goal change? Goals can be revised. Revision is not failure; forcing a goal that no longer fits is more likely to cause injury or loss of motivation.

## Feedback and records

A goal needs feedback. A training log can be simple: date, distance, duration, intensity, how it felt and any pain. Put process goals somewhere visible and tick them off, but do not turn ticks into a new source of pressure. When sharing a goal with a coach, friend or family member, say whether you want a reminder, company or technical advice.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide an individual training plan or guarantee a result. Goal setting does not replace medical assessment, coaching or real-life constraints. People with a chronic condition, injury history, pregnancy or a history of disordered eating should discuss goals with the relevant professional. Persistent anxiety, depression or a loss of control around training deserves psychological or medical support.
