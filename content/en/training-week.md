## Start with the number of runs you can repeat

The first question is not which perfect template to copy, but which days you can actually run. NHS Couch to 5K begins with three runs a week and rest days between them, which makes frequency part of the training structure. For most amateur runners, three or four runs a week is easier to sustain than “make up every missed day this week, then stop next week”. The right number still depends on your base, goal and recovery.

## Easy running takes most of the week

Easy running is not filler mileage. It accumulates aerobic stimulus and running volume at a manageable cost. The talk test still applies: full sentences are possible and you finish with something left. If you run three times a week, two easy runs plus one quality session or long run is a common shape; do not turn every run into race pace.

## Quality is a small slice

Tempo runs, intervals and hill sessions are quality work. They provide a stronger stimulus and cost more recovery. Beginners do not need to rush into them; experienced runners should place quality on a day when sleep, mood and weather are reasonably stable and avoid stacking hard days. CDC guidance also includes two days of muscle-strengthening activity a week, which can be arranged separately from running.

## Where the long run fits

The long run is a place to practise sustained movement, fuelling and route judgement, not a weekly maximum test. Its length should relate to your current base and goal, not someone else's kilometre count. Follow it with an easy day or rest; if you are still clearly fatigued the next day, do not add more next time.

## Keep a real recovery day

A recovery day can be complete rest or very light walking or jogging; the test is that it does not leave you more tired. Sleep, work and life stress change the cost of the same plan, so review once a week: which session was hardest to recover from? Which day is always skipped? Shrink the unsustainable part instead of forcing it with willpower.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not give an individual training plan or promise that a frequency suits your injury history, health or race goal. Pain, persistent fatigue, worsening sleep or a previous injury should prompt a lower load and assessment by a qualified coach or clinician.

## Sources and scope

Frequency and progression follow NHS Couch to 5K; adult activity and strength guidance follow the CDC; goal setting and weekly tracking follow the ODPHP planner. These are general public-health suggestions, not an individual prescription.
