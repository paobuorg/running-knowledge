## Fit comes first

A running shoe should fit. Leave about a thumb’s width in front of the toes, avoid pressure across the top of the foot, keep the heel from sliding up and down, and make sure the forefoot is not squeezed. Sizes differ between brands, so do not rely on the number alone. Feet swell slightly later in the day and after running; try shoes at a time close to when you actually run.

Wear the socks you run in, walk and jog a few steps in the shop, and pay attention to toes, arch and heel. New shoes do not need to be “broken in until they stop hurting”; if they pinch, rub or change how you walk from the start, try another pair. The NHS also suggests a fitting at a running shop while noting that expensive shoes are not essential.

## Cushioning and comfort

A secondary analysis of a randomised trial involving 527 recreational runners found that moderate or high perceived heel cushioning was associated with lower injury risk, and that higher overall shoe appreciation was also associated with lower risk. The study suggests that comfort and perceived cushioning may be useful cues when choosing shoes.

This is an association, not proof of cause. It does not show that switching to a softer shoe prevents injury, or that softer is always better. A very soft shoe can feel unstable to some runners, while a firmer shoe can feel harsh to others; individual response, surface, speed and training load all matter.

## Stability, support and “pronation”

Shoe shops often use “overpronation” to explain stability shoes, but judging it by eye or by sole wear is not reliable. Without pain or a functional problem, there is no need to force a “corrective” shoe because of a label. Recurrent sprains, marked instability, neurological symptoms or a structural foot problem deserve assessment by a sports-medicine or physiotherapy professional, not a shoe alone.

Shoes can change load distribution, but they do not replace strength, balance, movement and training adjustments.

## When to replace

There is no universal mileage. The NHS lists unsupportive or worn-out shoes among factors that can contribute to heel pain; practical signs include worn outsole, collapsed cushioning, a damaged upper, odour and reduced comfort when running. Rotating an older pair with a newer pair can help you feel the difference.

Tracking shoe mileage is useful, but a number is not a hard deadline. Do not wear new shoes or new socks for the first time in a race; long runs and races should use a combination you have already tested.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose foot type, gait or injury, or recommend a specific brand. Stop the relevant training and seek a qualified assessment for persistent pain, swelling, numbness, recurrent sprains or difficulty walking.
