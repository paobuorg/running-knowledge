## Weight is not the only performance variable

Running repeatedly lifts and decelerates body mass, so weight affects energy cost per kilometre. Performance also depends on training, endurance, pacing, running economy, course, weather and recovery. Treating weight as the only variable ignores training quality and health, and can turn normal fluctuation into a false failure.

CDC healthy-weight guidance emphasises balanced eating, regular activity and a sustainable lifestyle rather than short-term extreme dieting. For runners, enough carbohydrate and protein supports training and recovery; adding training while under-fuelling usually increases fatigue and injury risk.

## Risks of low energy availability

Chronic low energy availability is linked to RED-S and can affect endocrine, bone, metabolic, psychological and performance systems. Rapid weight loss, long-term low body weight, menstrual changes, recurrent stress fractures, persistent fatigue, feeling cold, poor sleep or loss of control around food are signals to take seriously.

Weight loss does not automatically mean better performance. Short-term loss may include glycogen and water; long-term under-fuelling can reduce recovery, bone density and immune function, eventually making training unsustainable.

## Race goals and weight

If a race goal involves changing weight, do it slowly and only when it does not compromise training or health, with a doctor or registered dietitian. Do not tie the race date to a weight-loss deadline; combining more training, less food and race pressure in the final weeks is one of the riskiest combinations.

Training days need enough carbohydrate for quality sessions and long runs; recovery days do not require deliberate hunger. Protein distribution, vegetables and total energy matter more than a “magic” food. Supplements do not replace normal eating or guarantee weight loss or speed.

## Body image and help

Seek eating-disorder or mental-health assessment for loss of control around food, weight or training; repeated weighing, secret eating, intense guilt after a meal, or weight change that affects health and social life. Earlier support is easier; do not wait for injury or missed periods.

## Scope and limits

This page does not provide a weight-loss plan, calorie target or body-fat standard, or diagnose eating disorders, RED-S or metabolic disease. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescents, chronic disease, diabetes or a history of disordered eating should discuss weight and training goals with a doctor or registered dietitian.

## Sources and scope

General healthy-weight and balanced-eating principles follow CDC healthy-weight guidance; the multi-system risks of low energy availability and RED-S follow the International Olympic Committee RED-S consensus page.
