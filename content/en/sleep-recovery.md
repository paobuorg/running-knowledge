## Sleep is not the opposite of training

Sleep supports repair, emotional regulation and daytime function. The NHS notes that sleep needs vary and that most adults need 7 to 9 hours; persistent sleep problems can affect daily life and may need medical advice. No training plan can cancel chronic sleep deprivation.

## Do not chase a perfect number

Seven to nine hours is a population range, not a nightly pass mark. Needs change with age, stress, illness, travel and training load. Rather than fixating on one night's score, look at a week: do you often struggle to fall asleep, wake during the night, wake unrefreshed, or notice daytime impairment?

## Adjust training around sleep

When sleep worsens for several days, reduce volume or drop a quality session and observe fatigue and mood; do not use more recovery gadgets or supplements to mask a sleep problem. A consistent wake time, less stimulation before bed and separating hard training from bedtime are common directions. A clinician or sleep specialist should assess causes and treatment.

## Before races and travel

Unfamiliar beds, early transport and nerves can affect sleep around a race. Plan travel, bring familiar sleep items and avoid changing every habit at the last minute. If the night before a race is poor, do not compensate with extra caffeine or unfamiliar supplements; a familiar race-day routine matters more.

## Scope and limits

This page does not diagnose insomnia, sleep apnoea or another sleep disorder, and does not recommend medication, supplements or a fixed sleep duration. Persistent insomnia, significant daytime impairment, snoring with breathing pauses or sudden daytime sleepiness should be medically assessed. Do not rely on willpower to fight drowsiness while driving or operating machinery.

## Sources and scope

Sleep duration, individual variation and the advice to seek help for persistent insomnia follow the NHS insomnia page. This is general recovery information, not diagnosis or individual treatment.
