## Surface is not a single-choice question

Running happens on roads, pavements, tracks, trails, grass, treadmills and mountain paths. Hardness, evenness, grip and variability differ, and the body adapts with different joint motion, muscle work and landing patterns. The goal is not to find a perfect surface but to match training load to current adaptation.

For most city runners, the most convenient surface is the most sustainable one. Travelling a long way to a softer trail every session is not automatically better than consistent running from home. Equally, training only on one surface and then entering a technical trail race exposes the body to unfamiliar load.

## What the research suggests

A small study of 30 male habitual rear-foot strikers compared running mechanics on artificial grass, rubber and concrete. It found no significant differences in several joint-motion and loading variables, but concrete was associated with lower braking and mediolateral forces, higher ankle moments and higher frontal-plane hip and knee moments. The authors suggested harder surfaces might increase overuse risk for rear-foot strikers, but the evidence is limited.

The sample was small and narrow, so it cannot be generalised to everyone or used to prove that concrete causes injury. It is better read as a reminder that surfaces redistribute load, and that “hard” versus “soft” is not the only dimension.

## Treadmill and outdoors

A treadmill gives controllable speed, incline and weather, and is useful for intervals, recovery runs and bad conditions. The belt moves underfoot and its compliance differs from outdoor ground, so cadence and stride may shift slightly. Start with a familiar speed and 0–1% incline, do not hang on the handrails to carry body weight, and avoid sudden steep inclines.

Outdoor surfaces vary more: pavements have seams and curbs, roads have traffic and camber, trails have stones and roots. Treat outdoor running as a task that requires reading the route, not just watching pace. At night, in rain or under leaves, surface judgement is harder; slow down and choose familiar routes.

## Changing surfaces safely

Change surface as you would change shoes or mileage: gradually. Add a few minutes of a new surface to an easy run, check the next day for unusual pain or tightness, then increase over several weeks. Technical trails also demand ankle stability, attention and navigation preparation; do not use one long run as a trial.

If the same body part complains repeatedly on one surface, do not reduce the explanation to “the ground is too hard”. Mileage, speed, shoes, recovery and movement all matter. Reducing the relevant load and tracking the response is more reliable than simply changing surface.

## Scope and limits

This page does not judge whether a specific path is safe, prescribe shoes or treat injury. Access rules for construction areas, private land, protected areas and roads are governed by local authorities and on-site signs.
