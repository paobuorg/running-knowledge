## Wind chill and hypothermia

The National Weather Service warns that extremely cold air combined with wind creates dangerous wind-chill values. People exposed to severe cold can develop frostbite and can become hypothermic within a short time. Uncovered skin and the extremities, such as hands and feet, are most vulnerable; hypothermia occurs when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it.

Before heading out, check not only temperature but also wind chill, precipitation, humidity and daylight. Strong wind, rain, snow and wet clothing raise the risk substantially. If conditions exceed your preparation, moving indoors or shortening the route is a sensible choice.

## Layers and sweat

The key is layering: a wicking base layer, an insulating layer and a wind- or water-resistant outer layer. Gloves, a hat or buff, warm socks and shoes with grip are often more useful than one very thick garment. Cotton holds water and dries slowly, which can make you colder.

Feeling slightly cool at the start is normal; open a zip or remove a layer as you warm up, and change into dry clothes soon after finishing. Do not overdress to “sweat more”, and do not test brand-new kit on race day.

## Ice, route and visibility

NPS winter hiking guidance includes using traction devices on ice and snow; carrying a map and compass or GPS rather than relying on a phone; checking weather and trail conditions; telling family or friends your route and expected return time; and remembering that snow can hide trail markers, so other people's tracks are not a reliable guide. Turn around when conditions deteriorate.

Urban runners also need to watch icy pavement, snow-clearing vehicles and shorter daylight. Choose familiar, well-lit routes with manageable traffic, plan to finish before dark, and wear reflective or bright clothing.

## Cold air and breathing

Cold air can irritate the airways and cause a dry throat, cough or breathing discomfort in some people. A longer warm-up, a buff or mask to soften the inhaled air, and lower intensity may help comfort, but these measures do not treat asthma or other respiratory disease.

If you have asthma, a heart condition, Raynaud's phenomenon or marked cold sensitivity, discuss winter exercise with a clinician first. Stop and seek urgent help for chest pain, severe breathlessness, confusion, violent shivering or difficulty speaking.

## What this page does not provide

This page does not provide field treatment for frostbite or hypothermia, or judge whether a specific temperature is safe for you. Severe frostbite, hypothermia, chest pain, altered consciousness or breathing difficulty are emergencies; contact local emergency services.
