## Night is not the daytime in a darker colour

Figures cited by the U.S. Federal Highway Administration note that almost half of pedestrian deaths occur between 6 p.m. and midnight, and that about eight out of ten drivers who struck pedestrians at night reported not seeing them. A pedestrian in dark clothing may first be seen only about 55 feet away, leaving very little time to react.

The practical lesson is that night running is first a visibility problem, not a pacing problem. Dark clothes, unlit roads, glare, rain, fog and parked cars all reduce detection. Seeing a car’s headlights does not mean the driver can see you.

## Reflective gear and light

Materials that retroreflect light back toward its source are more effective at night than ordinary light-coloured clothing. A reflective vest, armbands, ankle bands, reflective strips on shoes and a hanging tag on a pack can increase detection distance; placing them on moving body parts can help attract attention. In daylight, bright colours improve contrast.

Active light matters too. A headlamp or chest lamp helps you see the ground and helps oncoming traffic notice you; a red rear light helps traffic approaching from behind. Check batteries and carry a backup; cold and long use reduce runtime.

## Route and traffic rules

Choose lit, familiar routes with pavements, wider shoulders and lower speeds where possible. Cross at marked crossings and signals, and do not step out from between parked cars. At an unmarked crossing, wait until a vehicle has actually slowed or stopped before entering the road.

Running against traffic can make oncoming vehicles easier to see, but follow local traffic law and road conditions. Slow down near junctions, roadworks, bridges and slip roads, and never assume a driver will yield. Keep headphone volume low enough to hear traffic; use one earbud or transparency mode.

## Company and telling someone

Run with someone when possible, or tell a household member or friend your route and expected return time. Keep your phone charged; live location sharing is optional, but do not broadcast your real-time position publicly. If you are followed, harassed or involved in a collision, move toward a lit, populated place with people or cameras and contact local police or emergency services rather than running into an unfamiliar area to finish the route.

## Scope and limits

This page cannot guarantee that any route is safe at night and does not replace local traffic law, race rules or police guidance. Vision, hearing, balance or cardiovascular conditions can affect night safety; discuss exercise plans with a clinician if relevant. After a collision, head impact, altered consciousness or inability to bear weight, seek medical care.
