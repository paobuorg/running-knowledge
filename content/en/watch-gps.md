## GPS is an estimate, not a certified measurement

Watch GPS can drift or lose points under trees, near tall buildings, in valleys, tunnels and turns. It records the device's estimate, not a race-certified course. Use it for trends on the same route and for approximate pace and location, not as precise distance or official geometry. The National Park Service also warns that GPS can be unreliable and recommends alternative navigation.

## Optical heart rate has a delay

Wrist optical heart rate estimates from skin blood flow and can be affected by strap fit, cold, skin tone, tattoos, arm movement and sweat. It may lag during intervals, fast changes or strength work. A chest strap is often more stable but can also be disturbed. A single odd reading cannot diagnose a health problem; look at multi-day trends and perceived effort.

## Pace and distance are smoothed

Instant pace is often smoothed, and short reps, GPS drift or pauses can make it swing; average pace can also be affected by auto-laps and lost points. Cross-check with splits, route and perceived effort, and do not change your movement or ignore pain to chase a number.

## Battery, navigation and safety

For long runs and trails, battery, offline maps and a backup power source are part of safety kit. The National Park Service recommends a map/compass and headlamp; a phone or watch should not be the only navigation. Tell someone your route and return time. Device data cannot replace judgement about weather, closures and body signals.

## Scope and limits

This page does not rank brands or models, treat wrist heart rate as a medical diagnosis or guarantee GPS accuracy. Stop and seek medical help for chest pain, fainting, unusual palpitations or persistent symptoms rather than checking the watch again.

## Sources and scope

GPS reliability and alternative navigation follow the U.S. National Park Service Hike Smart and Ten Essentials pages; device accuracy and optical-heart-rate limits are general technical notes, not model-specific test results.
